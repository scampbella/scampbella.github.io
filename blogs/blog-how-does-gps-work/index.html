How Does GPS Work?

February 15, 2024 6 min read

The Global Positioning System is one of those technologies we use every single day without a second thought. Your phone knows exactly where you are, down to a few meters, anywhere on Earth. But the engineering behind this seemingly magical capability is a masterclass in physics, mathematics, and precise timing.

The Satellite Constellation

At the heart of GPS is a constellation of at least 24 satellites orbiting Earth at roughly 20,200 kilometers above the surface. These satellites are arranged in six orbital planes, ensuring that at any given moment, at least four satellites are visible from any point on the planet. Each satellite completes two orbits per day and broadcasts a signal containing its precise location and the exact time the signal was sent.

The time signals are generated by atomic clocks on board each satellite, accurate to within a few nanoseconds. Without this precision, the entire system would crumble. A timing error of just one microsecond translates to a positioning error of roughly 300 meters.

Trilateration: The Math of Positioning

Your GPS receiver doesn't actually communicate back to the satellites. Instead, it passively listens for signals and measures how long each signal took to arrive. Since radio waves travel at the speed of light, the time delay gives the distance to each satellite. With distances to at least four satellites, your receiver can solve for your exact latitude, longitude, altitude, and correct for any clock bias in the receiver itself.

What blows my mind is that this system also has to account for relativistic effects. The satellites are moving fast enough and are high enough in Earth's gravity well that both special and general relativity come into play. Without relativistic corrections, GPS would accumulate errors of about 10 kilometers per day. It's one of the few everyday technologies where Einstein's theories have a directly measurable impact on your life.
